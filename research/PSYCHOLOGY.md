# Buyer psychology: name, offer and price

Written 28 October 2026. Most studies below are small or about consumers, so treat them as direction, not proof. [n] refers to the sources at the end.

## Do this

1. **Name:** trade as **Sorted in a Week**, always with your name and face ("Sorted in a Week, by Alex Rapier"). Buy sortedinaweek.com and point doneinaweek.com at it.
2. **Price:** fixed **£2,950 a week**, shown as three options: 2 days £1,500, **1 week £2,950 (the one you push)**, 2 weeks £5,500. No day rate, no £2,999. 50% to book, 50% on Friday.
3. **Offer:** lead with the outcome ("the spreadsheet you run your business on, sorted"), use the week as proof, close with: "If what we agreed on Monday is not working by Friday, I keep going free until it is, or you do not pay the second half." Show the real next free week.
4. **Care plan:** first month free with every build, then **£200 a month (up to 3 hours of changes) or £2,000 a year** unless they say no. Cancel any time.
5. **Referrals:** at the Friday handover, once it works, ask "who else runs things off a spreadsheet?" and hand over a card: **their friend gets £250 off, they get a free month of care.**

## 1. Name

| Name | Trust | Easy to repeat | Caps the price? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alex Rapier | Highest: small clients trust a named person fastest [1] | Travels, but says nothing about what you do | No | Use as the byline |
| Alex Builds | Medium | Generic | Sounds like a hobby | No |
| Done in a Week | Medium | Clear, but "done" is about the task, not the buyer's problem | Slightly | Good, not best |
| **Sorted in a Week** | Medium, warm, British | Best: it is what a happy client already says, "he got it sorted in a week" | Slightly | **Pick** |
| Fixed in a Week | Lower | Easy | Yes: "fixed" means repairs, so you get small jobs | No |
| Week One Build | Lower, sounds like a course | Awkward | No | No |
| Five Days (.build) | Lowest: people will type .com | Vague | No | No |

Easy-to-say names are liked and remembered more [2], and most word of mouth is spoken, not online [3], so the name has to work out loud. "Sorted" is how UK owners say a problem has gone. It is too descriptive to trade mark [4], which does not matter at this size.

**Does "a week" signal cheap?** It can: people rate work higher when they think it took more effort [5]. So say why it is fast ("one client a week, nothing else"), and send a 2-minute video update at 5pm each day. Showing the work raises perceived value [6].

## 2. Pricing

**£2,000 is too low.** It is £400 a day, under the UK freelance developer median (£431 to £575) and a third below App in a Week at £2,995 (see MARKET.md). For an unknown seller, price is one of the few quality cues, and a lower price lowers what people expect [7].

**Fixed price, not a day rate.** A day rate invites comparison with every freelancer and punishes you for being quick. A fixed price is judged against the problem, not your hours.

**£2,950, not £2,999.** Round prices suit choices made on feel; odd prices suit choices made by calculation [8]. Owners buy a person on feel, so avoid retail-style £2,999. £2,950 stays "under three grand" and looks deliberate. Keep the 50% deposit: late payment hits 28% of UK businesses [9].

**Three options.** In Ariely's test, adding a third option moved most buyers to the dearer one [10], and people lean to the middle choice [11]. The 2-day option (£750 a day) makes the week look like the obvious deal and still pays well if picked. The 2-week option sets the anchor and catches bigger jobs.

**Revenue, not conversion.** At £2,000 a £100k pace needs 3.6 builds a month, leaving no time to sell. At £2,950, 2.5 builds plus 6 care plans gets there (£7,400 + £1,200 = £8,600 a month). Quote the next 5 leads at £2,950. If 4 or more say yes with no pushback, go to £3,500. If none say yes, fix the pitch before the price.

## 3. Offer framing

**Outcome first, speed as proof, guarantee to close.** Speed alone can read as rushed [5]. Lead with the pain in their words ("the admin you do on Sunday night").

**Guarantee: yes.** Guarantees help most when the seller is unknown and can backfire for strong brands [12]. You are the unknown seller. Tie "working" to the one-page spec signed on Monday. Free extra days come first to protect your cash; dropping the second half is the backstop.

**Scarcity, only if true.** Scarce things are valued more, most of all when demand caused it [13]. "One client a week. Next free week: w/c 17 November" is honest and works.

**One named job beats "I can do anything".** The site says "I build whatever your business needs", which makes the buyer do the imagining. Lead with spreadsheet to app for care agencies and trades; list websites and automation lower down.

## 4. Care plan

Keep **£200 a month**: it is already at the top of the normal band (MARKET.md), so make it an easy yes rather than dearer.

- **Default on.** Making pension sign-up automatic raised take-up sharply with nothing else changed [14]. Put it on the quote: "First month free, then £200 a month unless you tell me otherwise." Cancelling is one email, so it feels safe.
- **Annual option.** "Two months free" is the most common annual offer, and annual customers leave less [15]. It also brings cash in early.
- **Say what it buys:** hosting, backups, fixes and up to 3 hours of changes a month, so it does not look dear next to a £50 WordPress plan.

## 5. Referrals

Your first client came from a chat between people who knew each other. Referred customers are worth more and stay longer [16]. Between friends, rewards work best when some of it goes to the person being referred, and having a reward matters more than its size [17]. Rewarding both sides also stops the friend suspecting a pitch [18].

So the one mechanic: at the Friday handover, when they have just seen it work, ask "who else do you know who runs things off a spreadsheet?" and give them the card: friend gets £250 off, they get a free month of care. The month costs you hours, not cash, and the name gives them the line to repeat.
